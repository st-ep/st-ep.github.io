About

I am a Ph.D. student in robotics at UC Berkeley, advised by Krishna Kumar. I study how a robot can learn what a material is from a single touch, and then use what it learned to act.

I came to robotics through the ground. At Moscow State University I studied geology and measured how wet sand deforms and when it yields. At UT Austin I learned neural operators and the weak form of momentum balance. Now I bring the two together: world models that recover the physics of a material from one interaction and plan with it.

Research story

Dough, slime, glycerol, sand. The same push moves each one differently, and a robot that cannot tell them apart cannot plan. My work turns one interaction into a law the robot can reason with.

  1. i

    Observe one interaction

    The robot presses, bends or pours once. Cameras record how the material moves, and a force sensor, when the robot pushes, records how it pushes back.

  2. ii

    Recover the law

    A single least-squares solve on the weak form of momentum balance returns an explicit material law in seconds, with no differentiation through a simulator.

  3. iii

    Act on what was learned

    Held fixed, the law plans new actions on new shapes: pinching dough into a target, putting a golf ball with a soft club, pouring glycerol to a chosen volume.

Physically viable world models

The larger aim is a world model that can answer “what happens if I act this way?” by representing the physics behind the outcome, not only the look of the next frame.

Before robots, operators

Learning maps between functions from scattered, unordered sensors (SetONet), and inverting ill-posed ones with learned bases (B2B−1). The learned function bases from this line of work carry into the robot work.
